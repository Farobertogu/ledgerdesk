## 1 · Project context, in one paragraph

LedgerDesk is a **generic grounded-response platform**: any organisation can deploy it over its own document corpus to answer questions with cited sources — customer support, employee training, or any other body of documents that gets asked questions. It is **built generic and demonstrated specific**: the demonstration instance — the listed unit Project #4, *AI-Based Customer Support Automation System*, declared modified — loads a customer-support corpus for **"MidCo", a hypothetical case organisation**, an Australian B2B SaaS provider of roughly 40 employees and 1,200 accounts. In that instance, a customer raises a ticket in a portal and receives an immediate acknowledgement and a tracking number. Behind the portal, three sequential AI roles classify the ticket (category, severity, sentiment) and declare a confidence, draft a reply grounded in the corpus, and validate escalation; an explicit, LLM-free algorithm decides queue order and whether a case must reach a human now. A support agent sees the ticket with context gathered and a draft written, and **edits, approves and sends — always the last word**. Model weights are never retrained: what improves is the layer around the model (prompts, schemas, workflows, validators), and it improves from the edits agents make while doing their job; the corpus grows only by admitting material into **versioned, immutable snapshots** (decision dated 19 August 2026), so every answer stays tied to the version that produced it. Because a system that tunes itself against its own scoreboard will eventually report improvement that is not there, the reported accuracy is produced only from a **sealed evaluation split that the improvement loop never observes**. The business case rests on declared case assumptions — 850 tickets/month at 14.0 minutes average handling time, a loaded agent cost of A$38/h, giving an annual manual support cost of **≈ A$90,000 per year** (A$90.4k on the stated inputs) — and the `ASSUMED` status travels with the figure wherever it appears.

I am delivering this project under a **single-executor delivery model with functional role separation** (Section 5). That model, and the project itself, are submitted to the project facilitator for approval.

**What I cannot evidence today, and it is the same list every time.** My previous work has no automated tests, no continuous integration, no threat model, no accessibility verification, no usability evaluation with real participants, and no reproducible installation procedure. That is the difference between building software and engineering it, and closing it is the reason I am treating this capstone as a competence programme rather than a submission.

### 3.2 · The failure that shaped this plan

The most useful evidence about my current competence is a mistake I made **in my own prior research work** — not on LedgerDesk, which has measured nothing yet — and it is the reason one product requirement exists.

A measurement produced by a script named `k1_rho_d.py` appeared to be irreproducible: reading the script, its input directory was expected as a sibling of the script itself, and that directory was not in the working tree — a recursive search for the expected data files returned zero results. I concluded that the measurement could not be reproduced. **That conclusion was wrong.** The data existed in a different tree; the two copies of the script were byte-identical; the measurement re-runs unchanged. The lesson, in one line: *"it is not in this folder" ≠ "it does not exist"*. There had been no data loss. There had been a **provenance** failure — nothing on record tied the computation to the location of its input — and I nearly discarded a correct result because of it.

Two things follow, and both are in this plan. First, the diagnosis is honest about which competence failed: not the mathematics, but the record-keeping — a configuration-management gap (SFIA **CFMG**), not an analytical one. Second, the product inherits the fix: LedgerDesk's ledger records, per automated decision, the seed, the split, the hash **and the path of the input consumed**, so that the row itself says where to look. A design requirement that comes from a dated failure of my own is worth more to me than one copied from a textbook, and it is why I rate my provenance and configuration discipline low in the table below despite rating my analytical work highly.

**Two sources shape method rather than a single gap, and both changed a design decision.** The evaluation design of this project — a split that is sealed before the first run and a bounded budget of queries against it — comes from the adaptive data analysis result of Dwork *et al.* (2015): re-using an evaluation set adaptively inflates reported quality, and their holdout mechanism makes the size of that inflation an object one can reason about rather than hope about. The related phenomenon in automated search, where the search overfits its own validation signal late in a run, is documented by Zela *et al.* (2020), and the leaderboard mechanism of Blum and Hardt (2015) is the conceptual precedent for limiting adaptivity, although its guarantee is vacuous at the scale of this project and I therefore cite it as precedent only. Recent work re-evaluating automated harness improvement (Wang *et al.* 2026 — **a preprint that has not been peer reviewed**, presented non-archivally) supplies the operational rule I adopted: an improvement loop only counts as an improvement if it beats a comparable baseline at **equal budget**. That is why my evaluation plan includes a trivial baseline alongside every headline number.

### 5.1 · The delivery model

This project is delivered under a **single-executor delivery model with functional role separation**: six named roles, each with a defined responsibility set, decision rights and a hand-off artefact. The roles are held by one practitioner; **the separation is enforced by artefact, not by headcount**. I state this plainly because a model that is declared and verifiable is defensible, and a simulated team is not. The model is submitted to the project facilitator for approval together with the project.

**How the separation is made real rather than cosmetic.** Each role hands off an artefact that the next role can reject. Concretely: R3 promotes nothing that R4 has not certified against the sealed split, the split manifest is committed with a hash and a timestamp **before** evaluation begins, CI acts as the mechanical reviewer, and a design change that arrives without a dated decision record does not merge — a repository rule, not a memory. I am explicit about the limit of this arrangement: because one person holds all six roles on one machine, these controls **prevent accidental violation and detect deliberate violation; they are not a security boundary against an in-process adversary**. Stating that limit is part of the control, not a caveat on it.

## 6 · Professional responsibility and ethical considerations

Generic statements about ethics earn nothing. What follows is specific to this system, and each item names the control **and how it is verified**.

**Customer data privacy and PII redaction before third-party inference.** LedgerDesk sends ticket text to a commercial model provider. Three controls apply. (a) The prototype runs on **100% synthetic data generated from deterministic templates with ground truth** — a self-imposed constraint, declared in scope, that removes the risk rather than managing it. (b) Every provider call passes through a **single gateway**: no component may call the model outside it, redaction happens at that choke point, and a planted canary token that must never egress makes the control testable rather than assertable. (c) The data model carries an executable governance layer: a PII dictionary, a retention policy, and a purge job with a test that seeds expired rows and asserts that they are gone. Because the prototype processes no real personal information, the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) does not bind this build; the design is nevertheless written against the Australian Privacy Principles that would apply on deployment — principally **APP 11** (security of personal information) and **APP 8** (cross-border disclosure, which is precisely what a call to an overseas model provider is). The precise mapping of each control to the principle it satisfies is scheduled for verification against the regulator's guidance before the final report, and **I declare it as pending rather than claim compliance I have not verified**. One residual limit is stated openly and has no solution inside the prototype: the provider sees whatever the scaffolding sends it.

**Transparency toward the end user and the route to a human.** Customers are told that an AI is assisting, on every AI-assisted surface, and can always request a human — a request that creates a real escalation record with its own service-level clock, not a message into a void. The stronger control is structural: the system **cannot send or close a customer-facing response without a recorded human approval**, and this is enforced as a database constraint rather than a workflow convention, so application code cannot bypass it. Under ISO/IEC 25010:2023 this is a **safety** requirement — operational constraint — which is the classification I use, because the 2023 edition of the standard added safety as a characteristic and it is where non-bypassable human approval properly belongs (ISO/IEC 2023). The agentic risk framework's **least agency** principle is the design rule I follow for what the evolvable layer is permitted to touch: prompts, schemas, workflows and validators, and never the model weights, the escalation rule or the sealed evaluation data (OWASP Foundation 2025).

**Bias in automatic ticket classification.** Automated triage decides who is answered first. That makes disparity a foreseeable harm, not a hypothetical one. The system therefore runs a **bias probe across cohorts** — service tier, locale, message length, and name substitution — on escalation and priority outcomes, and publishes the disparity **with confidence intervals rather than as a point estimate**, every release. Two honesty constraints attach. The published work on cross-cultural variation among human evaluators (Kirk et al. 2024) is *about evaluators*, not about ticket triage; where I use it, the transfer to fairness in triage is **my inference and is written as one**. And a bias probe is a detector, not a remedy: it tells me a disparity exists, and the decision about what to do about it belongs to a human, which is why the classifier's outputs are features for an explicit, auditable algorithm and never the decision itself.

**Intellectual property.** Library licences are attributed, the repository carries a declared licence, and no third-party dataset is used. Code enters the repository only through the same gate as any other change — a test that exercises it, a decision record when it changes a design, and my signature on the commit. All factual claims and every reference in my deliverables are checked against the source document; the project keeps a references file recording, per reference, the citation, the resolvable identifier, the access date and a verification tick confirming the source itself was inspected, and **no reference enters a deliverable without it**. The judgement, the decisions and the errors are mine.

**Accessibility.** Accessibility is built, not retro-fitted: the three principal flows target **WCAG 2.2 level AA** (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C] 2024) — the current Recommendation, also adopted as an ISO standard — verified by an automated checker running in CI on every build. I word the resulting claim exactly as the evidence permits: *no automated violations detected on the three principal flows, plus manual verification of keyboard operation, focus order and contrast against named success criteria*. **That is not a conformance audit**, and I do not describe it as one. Under ISO/IEC 25010:2023 this requirement is classified as *inclusivity*, a sub-characteristic of interaction capability, which is what gives accessibility a formal home in the quality model rather than a paragraph of goodwill (ISO/IEC 2023).

**Information security.** Security is designed against a threat catalogue written for this class of system — agentic, tool-using, with persistent memory (OWASP Foundation 2025) — and expressed as a STRIDE-lite table with **one threat, one control and one test per component**: role-based access with row-level security by tenant and by role; append-only ledger and audit tables with a hash chain, so truncation is detectable; rate limiting and an API budget circuit breaker; secrets kept out of the repository; and a dependency audit in CI. Residual risks are named alongside their controls rather than omitted — no multi-factor authentication in the prototype, a server clock that is not third-party stamped, and provider-side limits outside my control.

**Honesty of measurement as a professional obligation.** The sealed evaluation split is the ethical centre of this project, not merely its technical one. A support manager who deploys an assistant and is asked "is it actually right?" should not have to rely on the vendor's impression. The reported number therefore comes only from data the improvement loop never observes, and the number is published with its sample size, its interval, its seal hash and the count of queries spent against a pre-committed budget. **A null result is a deliverable, not a failure**: if the interval crosses zero, I publish the minimum detectable effect instead of a favourable subset. Choosing in advance to be able to report that the system did not improve is, in my view, the specific professional obligation that automated self-improvement creates.
